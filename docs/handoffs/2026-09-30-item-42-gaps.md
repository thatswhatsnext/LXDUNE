### 1. `poe`: append worked sequence `ep-s4-freeze-thaw` to `workedSequences`

Focus area: Change (Stage 4), the geological half. Append after the guide's last existing worked sequence, before `misapplications:`, with one blank line between sequences.

```yaml
  - id: ep-s4-freeze-thaw
    title: Can water break a rock?
    context: { stages: [stage4], focusAreas: [Change], disciplines: [earth-environmental] }
    contentOutcomes: [SC4-CHG-01]
    bigQuestion: How can something as soft as water break something as hard as rock?
    learningIntentions:
      - Water expands as it freezes, which is unusual; most substances contract as they cool
      - Freezing changes volume but not mass
      - Repeated freezing and thawing of water in cracks breaks rock apart, as energy is removed and added over and over
    targetConceptions:
      - Everything shrinks when it gets cold
      - Ice is heavier than the water it came from
      - Rock is too hard for water to break
    predictPrompt: Before reading on, students predict the frozen bottle and weigh it in the same lesson. Why ask about mass as well as shape, when the lesson is about breaking rock?
    steps:
      - minutes: "0–5"
        phaseId: predict
        activity: |
          Two identical plastic bottles, each filled to the brim with water and capped. The teacher says one
          went into the freezer yesterday afternoon and is still there. Every student writes what that bottle
          will look like now, whether it will weigh more, less or the same as this one, and why.
        wsOutcomes: [SC4-WS-02]
        formativeCheck: Written predictions with reasons, collected unseen
        scaffoldSlots:
          - field: activity
            prompt: A student predicts the frozen bottle will be heavier "because ice is solid". What does that tell you, and which part of the Observe phase will test it?
      - minutes: "5–10"
        phaseId: predict
        activity: |
          The class tallies predictions for shape (smaller, the same, bigger) and for mass, without discussion.
          Most will predict it shrinks, because cooling makes things contract.
        wsOutcomes: [SC4-WS-02]
        formativeCheck: Class tallies on the board
      - minutes: "10–25"
        phaseId: observe
        activity: |
          The frozen bottle comes out: bulging, the cap forced up, a dome of ice standing proud of the rim. Both
          bottles go on the balance in turn. Then a plastic measuring cylinder, marked at its water level before it
          was frozen, shows how far the ice has risen. Students record shape, mass and the two levels in an
          observation column, separate from what they think it means.
        wsOutcomes: [SC4-WS-01, SC4-WS-05]
        formativeCheck: Observation tables checked for measurements and for separation of observation and inference
      - minutes: "25–42"
        phaseId: explain
        activity: |
          Pairs write what they predicted, what they saw and what would have to be true for both. The teacher
          consolidates: water expands by about a tenth as it freezes, because ice forms an open structure; the
          mass stays the same because nothing was added or taken away. She names the everything-shrinks idea
          rather than leaving it unspoken. Then a photo of shattered rock on an alpine slope: pairs explain what
          water in a crack would do on a frosty night, and what happens when it thaws and freezes again.
        wsOutcomes: [SC4-WS-06, SC4-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: Each pair's explanation of the alpine photo, shared and compared
      - minutes: "42–50"
        phaseId: explain
        activity: 'Exit ticket: "I predicted… I observed… Now I think… A rock with water in its cracks, after a hundred frosty nights, would…"'
        wsOutcomes: [SC4-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: Exit tickets sorted to plan the next lesson
    whyItWorks:
      - The prediction runs against a rule students already trust, that cooling makes things shrink, so the result cannot be absorbed without thinking
      - Weighing both bottles separates volume from mass, which the heavier-ice idea confuses
      - The measuring cylinder turns a dramatic effect into a quantity, so "about a tenth" is something they saw
      - The alpine photo carries the result into geology, so the lesson answers the question it opened with
    watchFor: |
      Freezing takes hours, so the frozen bottle and cylinder are prepared the day before, and a spare of
      each is worth having in case one has leaked. Glass must not be used: it can shatter in the freezer.
      This episode covers the geological half of Change. The chemical half is `ep-s4-steel-wool`; run the two
      together and ask students where the energy went in each.
    safetyNotes: |
      This guide does not carry safety advice and is not a substitute for your school's risk assessment.
      What this sequence introduces, so you know what to assess: sealed plastic containers frozen until they
      split, which can leave sharp edges; ice handled by students; and water on the floor near the balance.
      Take these to your head teacher or faculty safety contact and check them against your school's practical
      procedures and current NSW guidance before you run anything.

      One point specific to POE. You supply the event, so you know in advance exactly what will be in the
      room. Make sure the event does not also surprise you: freeze a test bottle first, in the same freezer.
    provenance: { source: ai-drafted-reviewed, authors: ["Claude"], reviewedBy: ["Steve Grant"], reviewedOn: 2026-09-30 }
```

### 2. `adi`: append worked sequence `seq-s5-sound` to `workedSequences`

Focus area: Waves and motion (Stage 5), the waves half. Append after the guide's last existing worked sequence, before `misapplications:`, with one blank line between sequences.

```yaml
  - id: seq-s5-sound
    title: Which material would keep the noise out of a classroom?
    context: { stages: [stage5], focusAreas: [Waves and motion], disciplines: [physics] }
    contentOutcomes: [SC5-WAM-01]
    bigQuestion: Which material best reduces the sound reaching a classroom, and does the answer depend on the sound?
    learningIntentions:
      - Sound is a wave that transfers energy through a medium; its amplitude relates to loudness and its frequency to pitch
      - When sound meets a material it is reflected, absorbed or transmitted, in proportions that depend on the material and the frequency
      - A scientific argument links a claim to analysed evidence with a justification
    targetConceptions:
      - Sound cannot travel through solids
      - Soft materials always block sound best
      - Loudness and pitch are the same thing
    predictPrompt: Before reading on, the guiding question ends "does the answer depend on the sound?". What would be lost from the argumentation session if it stopped at "which material"?
    steps:
      - lessons: "1"
        phaseId: task-question
        activity: |
          Introduce the guiding question and the set-up: a small speaker playing a steady tone inside a box whose
          lining can be changed, and a sound-level meter app on a phone at a fixed distance outside it. Tool talk
          on the tone generator, reading decibels, and keeping the volume moderate. Display the argument
          criteria.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-02]
        formativeCheck: Each group states what evidence would answer the question
      - lessons: "2"
        phaseId: design-data
        activity: |
          Groups choose materials to compare from what is available (foam, felt, cardboard, bubble wrap,
          timber, egg cartons) and decide how to keep thickness, distance and volume fair, how many readings to
          take, and which frequencies to test. The teacher conferences each plan for safety and feasibility,
          asking about fairness without correcting the design.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-03]
        formativeCheck: Plan approval conference
        scaffoldSlots:
          - field: formativeCheck
            prompt: A group plans to test one frequency only. What would you ask, without telling them the result depends on it?
      - lessons: "3"
        phaseId: design-data
        activity: |
          Groups collect readings with and without each lining, at their chosen frequencies, and record the
          reduction in decibels, noting anything else that could affect the meter, such as other groups' noise.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-04, SC5-WS-05]
        formativeCheck: Data tables checked mid-collection for repeats and a no-lining baseline
      - lessons: "4"
        phaseId: tentative-argument
        activity: |
          Groups analyse their data, choosing how to represent the reduction for each material and frequency,
          and build a whiteboard argument with claim, evidence and justification.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-05, SC5-WS-06]
        formativeCheck: Whiteboard check for all three parts
      - lessons: "5"
        phaseId: argumentation
        activity: |
          Round robin: one presenter stays with each whiteboard while others circulate and critique using
          displayed stems. Groups that tested different frequencies are paired on purpose. Groups log the
          questions asked and the changes they make.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: Record of questions and revisions
      - lessons: "6"
        phaseId: reflective-discussion
        activity: |
          Whole-class discussion consolidates the wave ideas: amplitude and loudness, frequency and pitch,
          and reflection, absorption and transmission, using the class's results. It addresses the idea that
          soft always wins, and connects to applications students have met: noise barriers beside motorways,
          hearing protection, and ultrasound imaging, which depends on sound reflecting from boundaries.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-06, SC5-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: Exit ticket on one wave feature and on what made an argument stronger
      - lessons: "7"
        phaseId: report
        activity: Each student writes an individual report answering the guiding question, within a word limit, including a recommendation for a real classroom.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: Draft justification paragraph checked
      - lessons: "8"
        phaseId: peer-review
        activity: |
          Anonymised reports are reviewed by another group against the review guide. The teacher samples
          reviews and returns any that are not specific enough.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: Sampled reviews
        scaffoldSlots:
          - field: activity
            prompt: Which three criteria would you put on the review guide for this investigation, and why those three?
      - lessons: "8"
        phaseId: revision
        activity: Authors revise, respond to each review point, and submit the final report with the review attached.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: Draft and final compared against the shared criteria
    whyItWorks:
      - The question is open enough that groups reach different defensible conclusions, especially across frequencies
      - Design choices are the students', so critique about fairness and repeats lands on their own decisions
      - The soft-always-wins idea surfaces in critique, where data from other groups can challenge it
      - Applications come after the investigation, so they arrive as uses of an idea students have evidence for
    watchFor: |
      Decibels are a logarithmic scale, so a fall from 80 to 70 is not "a bit less" and averages of decibel
      readings mislead. Decide before lesson 4 whether to raise this or to have groups compare reductions
      only, and be consistent in the reflective discussion.
      This sequence covers waves only. Motion, the other half of the focus area, is `seq-s5-motion` in the 5E
      guide.
    safetyNotes: |
      This guide does not carry safety advice and is not a substitute for your school's risk assessment.
      What this sequence introduces, so you know what to assess: sustained tones from several speakers in one
      room, where volume matters for hearing and for students with sound sensitivity; students' own phones
      used as instruments; and handling of insulation materials, some of which shed fibres. Take these to
      your head teacher or faculty safety contact and check them against your school's practical procedures
      and current NSW guidance before you run anything.

      One point specific to ADI. Groups design their own data collection, so the methods are not known when
      you write the risk assessment. Setting a maximum volume and an approved materials list before lesson 2
      gives groups the limits as part of the problem rather than as a rule imposed after they have planned.
    provenance: { source: ai-drafted-reviewed, authors: ["Claude"], reviewedBy: ["Steve Grant"], reviewedOn: 2026-09-30 }
```

### 3. `swh`: append worked sequence `swh-seq-s5-duckweed` to `workedSequences`

Focus area: Environmental sustainability (Stage 5), SWH's first Biology sequence. Append after the guide's last existing worked sequence, before `misapplications:`, with one blank line between sequences.

```yaml
  - id: swh-seq-s5-duckweed
    title: What does run-off do to a waterway?
    context: { stages: [stage5], focusAreas: [Environmental sustainability], disciplines: [biology] }
    contentOutcomes: [SC5-ENV-01]
    bigQuestion: How do substances that humans wash into waterways affect the living things in them, and how would we know?
    learningIntentions:
      - Human activity changes water quality, and living things respond to those changes in measurable ways
      - A change that helps one organism can harm the ecosystem around it
      - A claim about impact is defended with selected, measured evidence, and revised when compared with others and with published findings
    targetConceptions:
      - A pollutant always makes living things grow less
      - If something is not visible in the water, it has no effect
      - One container of duckweed is enough to show an effect
    predictPrompt: Before reading on, some groups will find that fertiliser makes the duckweed grow faster. Why is that the most useful result the class could get?
    steps:
      - lessons: "1"
        phaseId: beginning-ideas
        activity: |
          Groups look at duckweed under hand lenses, then at photos of the same creek after heavy rain and in a
          dry spell. Each student writes what they would want to find out about how things washed off roads,
          gardens and homes affect life in the creek. Questions are conferenced briefly for testability and kept.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-02]
        formativeCheck: Each student's written question, checked for investigability
      - lessons: "2"
        phaseId: tests
        activity: |
          Groups design a test using duckweed in cups of pond or aged tap water, with substances from an approved
          list at chosen concentrations: garden fertiliser, dishwashing detergent, and table salt. Plans name what
          will be counted (fronds), how often, how many cups, and where they will be kept. The teacher conferences
          each plan for safety and feasibility without redesigning it.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-03]
        formativeCheck: Plan conference before duckweed is issued
        scaffoldSlots:
          - field: formativeCheck
            prompt: A group has one cup for each substance and no cup with plain water. What do you ask, without telling them what is missing?
      - lessons: "3–4"
        phaseId: observations
        activity: |
          Over about two weeks, groups count fronds at the start of each science lesson and note colour and
          condition, recording what they see separately from what they think it means. Lessons 3 and 4 fall at
          the end of that period, when groups finish counting and organise their data.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-01, SC5-WS-04, SC5-WS-05]
        formativeCheck: Count records checked at each lesson for consistency and separation from interpretation
      - lessons: "5"
        phaseId: claims
        activity: |
          Each student writes an individual claim answering their own beginning question, in a form another
          student could disagree with.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-06]
        formativeCheck: Each claim read for whether it could be argued with
      - lessons: "5"
        phaseId: evidence
        activity: |
          Students select the counts that support their claim, say why they support it, and account for anything
          that does not fit, such as one cup that behaved differently from its repeats.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-06, SC5-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: The link between claim and data, read on every student's work
      - lessons: "6"
        phaseId: reading
        activity: |
          Groups pair up and compare claims, working out where they differ and why. Only then does the teacher
          release a short reading on nutrient run-off and algal blooms, and on salinity in Australian waterways.
          Groups that found fertiliser helped the duckweed revise their claim about impact in the light of what
          a bloom does to the rest of a waterway. Revisions are written, not just discussed.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-07, SC5-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: Record of where each student agreed, disagreed and revised
      - lessons: "6"
        phaseId: reflection
        activity: |
          Beginning questions are handed back. Students write what they first thought pollution did, what
          changed their mind, and one change to what goes down drains at home or school that their evidence
          supports.
        wsOutcomes: [SC5-WS-08]
        formativeCheck: Written comparison of beginning ideas and final claim
    whyItWorks:
      - Fertiliser can increase duckweed growth, so "impact" has to be argued about the ecosystem, not just one plant
      - Claims come from the students' own counts, so the reading in lesson 6 has something to be set against
      - Repeated cups make variation visible, which gives the evidence phase real work to do
      - Beginning questions are kept from lesson 1, so the reflection has something to compare against
    watchFor: |
      The two-week growth period sits across other lessons, so counting takes five minutes at the start of each
      science lesson rather than a lesson of its own. Plan the unit around it, and keep the cups in one place
      with even light, or light will decide the result.
      Lesson 5 is where the teacher's time goes, asking one student at a time why their counts support what
      they wrote.
    safetyNotes: |
      This guide does not carry safety advice and is not a substitute for your school's risk assessment.
      What this sequence introduces, so you know what to assess: household chemicals (fertiliser and
      detergent) handled and diluted by students; pond water, if used, and hygiene after handling it; and the
      disposal of duckweed and treated water. Take these to your head teacher or faculty safety contact and
      check them against your school's chemical and practical procedures and current NSW guidance before you
      run anything.

      A biosecurity point as well as a safety one: duckweed and treated water must not go into drains,
      gardens or waterways at the end. Agree the disposal method before the plants arrive; it is also a fair
      question to put to the class, given what the sequence is about.

      One point specific to SWH. Students design the method from their own question, so the hazards are not
      fully known when you write the risk assessment. The approved substance list is the limit, and it belongs
      to the students at the plan checkpoint.
    provenance: { source: ai-drafted-reviewed, authors: ["Claude"], reviewedBy: ["Steve Grant"], reviewedOn: 2026-09-30 }
```
